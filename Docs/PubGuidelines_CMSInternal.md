## Introduction
The CMS Publications Committee (PubComm) is responsible for all articles submitted to a journal that are signed by the CMS Collaboration. The PubComm has a mandate, specified in the CMS Constitution, to ensure that all CMS papers are of high quality and adhere to high editorial standards. The Constitution also mandates that the PubComm provide and maintain these style guidelines. Drafts of publication documents should be carefully checked against these guidelines before being submitted to a collaboration wide review (CWR). To speed up the process, it is recommended that these guidelines be consulted before the first draft of a paper is started.

The PubComm also oversees CMS conference reports, as well as CMS notes that are to become public. However, the quality of these documents is the responsibility of their authors: either individuals or a subgroup of the collaboration. Therefore, the guidelines described in this document can be followed in a more relaxed manner for conference reports and notes.

These guidelines are intended to reflect modern scientific editing practice. Firstly, they promote a uniform style for CMS preprints and publications, making the body of our work more professional in appearance, easier to digest, and more manifestly the product of the collaboration -- carefully scrutinized and approved at a high level -- rather than results from a mere handful of analysts. Secondly, they address mistakes that commonly appear in the preparation of physics documents as well as issues that often arise in CWRs and final readings. The early correction of these issues will speed up the publication process. Finally, they provide a set of conventions, mostly the product of consensus between a group of experienced CMS editors, that are intended to minimize the level of contradictory requests made during the review process and thus also to streamline the publication process. These "Guidelines" are for guidance. They should be seen as reasonable and well justified recommendations. Deviations from them are allowed with good reason. In the case of doubt regarding the application of any guideline, **clarity is the overriding principle**.

Guidelines on the technical preparation of documents are given [here](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/bin/view/CMS/Internal/PubPreparation). See also the explanation of the CMS document repository system given in [Ref. \[1![](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/pub/TWiki/TWikiDocGraphics/external-link.gif)](https://gitlab.cern.ch/tdr/utils/raw/master/general/notes_for_authors.pdf)\] below, and a sample paper template given in [Ref. \[2![](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/pub/TWiki/TWikiDocGraphics/external-link.gif)](https://gitlab.cern.ch/tdr/papers/XXX-08-000/raw/master/XXX-08-000_temp.pdf)\]. The CMS LaTeX symbol definitions and particle pennames are given at the ends of both of these documents.

Please send comments, complaints, or additional points to the [CMS Publications Committee chair.](mailto:cms-publication-committee-chair@cern.ch?subject=%22Guidelines%20for%20Authors%20comment%22)

## Guidelines for the general style of CMS publications
CMS documents intended for publication should be scrutinized for proper English grammar, spelling, and usage before being submitted to the collaboration wide review. The authors and editors should follow the present guidelines where applicable. A document that has not been edited for English may be returned to the submitter for corrections. The authors should not expect that the PubComm will necessarily perform the English editing or provide specific feedback on each page of the document.

The text should be complete, accurate, written in good comprehensible English, and edited for clarity and readability. Conciseness is strongly encouraged, but clarity and consistency are more important. Ambiguity should be avoided and the language chosen so as to be clear to nonnative English speakers. Use familiar words that are as equally effective as uncommon ones. Do not use an abundance of words if a few will do, and avoid complicated sentence structures. Keep jargon and acronyms to a minimum, and define all symbols. Think twice before emphasizing text (italics, etc.); it can be distracting and is not recommended. Divide the text into sections and paragraphs: introduce an idea, discuss it, and then conclude. Do not surprise the reader: introduce a concept before discussing it.

The title should be concise and give a clear, precise synopsis of the subject matter. The abstract should be clear, include CMS (accompanied by either "Collaboration", "experiment", or "detector") and either "LHC" or "CERN LHC", state the type of beams, the center-of-mass energy, the integrated luminosity, and give the main result(s), including statistical and systematic uncertainties if appropriate. Stating the calendar year(s) in which the data were collected is appropriate for archival purposes, to better document the data set. In the introduction, state the physics objectives and motivation for the study, mention existing measurements, standard model expectations, etc. The introduction should also mention CMS and LHC (or "CERN LHC"), as in the abstract. In the body of the text, make sure the numbers that are the outcome of the measurement are clearly stated before using these numbers to derive conclusions. Succinctly summarize the results and conclusions in a "Summary" section.

Each of the journals to which we submit papers have their own conventions for such things as writing "Table 1" versus "Tab. 1", the formatting of references, etc. Do not try to adjust your paper to meet these conventions. Once the paper is accepted, the journal copy editors will make all the required changes to meet their conventions. Instead, follow the formats and conventions described in this style guide. Doing so will give our preprints a uniform style.

## Self-plagiarism
Do not copy word-for-word text that has been used in an earlier CMS article that is published in a refereed journal, especially in the introductory section. This is considered bad practice, can lead to copyright issues, and many journals strongly disapprove of it. Paraphrase similar sentences and refer to the earlier publication instead of restating the same ideas. This rule does not apply to the CMS detector description, for which you are encouraged to use (with adaptation, if appropriate) the standard [CMS detector description twiki page](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/bin/viewauth/CMS/Internal/PubDetector) sentences.

## Language and spelling
### British versus American English
We let the authors decide whether to use British or American English conventions in writing their paper drafts. American conventions are widely used in PLB, JHEP, and EPJC, so there is no need to use British English for these journals. The APS journals (PRL, PRD, PRC) use American English only, and the copy editors will make the switch to American conventions for papers submitted to them with British conventions. Whichever you choose, the important thing is to follow those conventions consistently throughout the paper.

Avoid usage that may have different meanings on opposite sides of the Atlantic; for example "quite difficult" means "very difficult" to Americans but "rather difficult" or "mildly difficult" to British.

British conventions lead to different spellings compared to American conventions for certain words commonly found in our papers such as "colour", "flavour", "favour", and "centre" (see the [appendix](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/bin/view/CMS/Internal/PubGuidelines#USvsUK) at the end of this document for more examples). There is a common misunderstanding about words containing "ize", like "normalize", "maximize", "digitize", "realize", "recognize", and "minimize", as well as the corresponding nouns such as "normalization", "maximization", "digitization", etc . Traditional British English aligns with American English in advocating "ize", although in most cases "ise" is considered to be an acceptable alternative in British English. However, scientific writing favours "ize". Therefore, we encourage using the "ize" spelling. However, note that words not derived from Greek, such as "revise", do not follow this pattern. Also, some words, such as "comprise" and "compromise", are always spelled with an "s". Finally, proper names do not change depending on convention, e.g. "Aspen Center for Physics", "International Centre for Theoretical Physics".

The words "parametrize" and "parametrization" can also follow this "ize" preference. But they have the additional confusion of also being spelled as "parameterize" and "parameterization". There is a slight preference in American English for the "tri" form and in British English for the "ter" form. However, both forms are acceptable in American and British English conventions. So choose either form. As always, consistency throughout a paper is the most important goal.

In British English there is a comma before "i.e." and "e.g." but no comma after. Be sure to use the "\\ie" and "\\eg" macros to avoid an end-of-sentence space following the trailing period. In American English, there is a comma both before and after "i.e." and "e.g.", and you should write "\\ie," and "\\eg,".

In British English, use "anticlockwise" while in American English use "counterclockwise".

The spell checker in MS Word can be set to British or American English in the Tools menu (select Language). In case of doubt, spelling can be checked using [dictionary.com![](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/pub/TWiki/TWikiDocGraphics/external-link.gif)](http://dictionary.reference.com/), [Collins Dictionary![](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/pub/TWiki/TWikiDocGraphics/external-link.gif)](https://www.collinsdictionary.com/), the [Oxford English Dictionary![](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/pub/TWiki/TWikiDocGraphics/external-link.gif)](http://www.oed.com/), etc.

In linux, you can check the spelling of your paper (written in LaTeX) with the commands:  
`**ispell -d american paper.tex**`  
`**ispell -d british paper.tex**` (except that both flavor and flavour are accepted)

## Grammar and punctuation
### Passive versus active voice, and present versus past tense
While we strongly encourage a mixture of passive and active voice in the body of the paper, to keep the paper from becoming monotonous, the Abstract and the Summary (or Conclusions) should be written using passive voice.

Use of the present tense is encouraged throughout the paper to keep it active and more engaging for the reader, with occasional use of the past tense where appropriate, such as in phrases like "The data were collected with the CMS detector" or "The chamber was filled with gas". Use the present tense in the abstract, e.g. "Results are presented...". In the Summary (or Conclusions) we should use the present perfect tense ("Results have been presented"), the past tense, ("Events were selected"), or the present tense ("The measured cross section is").

### Commas
Punctuation should be used to clarify meaning. Generally, commas are used before and after clauses and phrases that could be omitted without changing the meaning of the sentence. However, it is not easy to formulate a complete set of general rules for the use of commas, and misuse or lack of commas can change the meaning of a sentence or produce confusion. The overuse of commas can break up the flow of a sentence, while too few can lead to ambiguity. Overall improvement to clarity is paramount.

**Serial comma.** In a list of three or more items, use a comma before the conjunction "and" or "or". The serial comma (sometimes called the Oxford or Harvard comma) reduces the risk of ambiguity in a sentence. Consider the meaning of the following sentence, with and without the serial comma:
-   We are indebted to our technicians, Michel Della Negra and Jim Virdee.

**Semicolons as item separators.** For complex list structures, such as lists in which items contain a comma, an "and", or are phrases, consider using semicolons as item separators, to improve clarity.

**i.e., e.g., and et al.** In American English, the rule is to use a comma both before and after "i.e." and "e.g.". In British English, the rule is to have a comma before but not after. No comma should precede "et al." in either convention. Also, do not write any of these in italics. Use the macros \\ie, \\eg, and \\etal.

### Hyphens
Hyphens should be used to increase clarity, but there is no universally accepted set of hyphenation rules. The trend in scientific writing is to avoid a hyphen when it does not serve a useful purpose, e.g. "pileup", "top quark decays", "b quark jet". The recommendations given here are intended for guidance. As always, improved clarity is the overriding principle in case of doubt. Authors should consider that reorganizing a sentence may be a better way to resolve an ambiguity than adding a hyphen.

**Use a hyphen**  
The following should be hyphenated as attributive adjectives (an attributive adjective is an adjective that comes before the noun that it is describing or qualifying):
-   A noun + present or past participle: "gas-filled chambers", "English-speaking people", "U-shaped tube";
-   Some combinations form passive verb forms, so they must be hyphenated to show they are performing as a unit, e.g. "Doppler-shifted", "Fourier-transformed";
-   An adjective + present or past participle: "good-sized sample", "straight-sided cavern", "fine-grained texture";
-   "Well", "ill", or "little" + past participle: "well-known theorem", "ill-defined terms", "little-known result", but do not hyphenate if the adjective is modified by an adverb: "very well known theorem", "extremely fine grained texture";
-   A preposition + noun or adjective: "near-surface reaction", "near-death experience", "next-to-leading-order calculation" (but "calculated to next-to-leading order");
-   Temporary compounds formed by an adjective + noun indicating number, dimension, or quality: "single-photon approximation", "three-component gas", "nth-order equation", "low-\\pt region".  

If a combination that would normally require a hyphen is modified by an adverb, a hyphen is not used: "high-\\pt particles", but "very high \\pt particles".

**Do NOT use a hyphen**  
The following combinations should NOT be hyphenated as attributive adjectives:
-   An irregular comparative or superlative + participle or noun: "best known result", "least known theorem";
-   Foreign phrases: "a priori solution", "in situ technique";
-   Adverbs ending in "ly" + adjective or participle: "slowly flowing gas", "highly complex approach", "oppositely charged particles";
-   Chemical compounds: "sulfuric acid bath", "carbon dioxide gas", "lead tungstate crystals";
-   Compounds indicating direction or placement: "upper right corner", "north central Switzerland";
-   Temporary compounds used as attributive adjectives formed by a noun + noun: "energy flow region", "wake surface potential", "jet energy corrections";
-   Permanent compounds (frequently used word combinations) that have their own (unhyphenated) dictionary entries: "Higgs boson", "Z boson", "W boson", "top quark", "cross section", "standard model", "black hole", "cosmic ray", "magnetic field", "form factor", "maximum likelihood fit" ;
-   Other combinations of an adjective + noun that may be considered permanent because of their common usage in our field: "b quark", "b tagging", tau lepton, "$S$ wave";
-   Comparative modifiers using "more" or "less" do not need hyphens except on the rare occasions when the meaning is ambiguous without one, for example, "the more common approach", "the less known algorithm".

**Usually use a hyphen**  
The following category of attributive adjectives usually take a hyphen, but some recommendations or journal guidelines will differ:
-   Phrases that act as attributive modifiers: "signal-to-noise ratio", "order-of-magnitude estimate", "tag-and-probe procedure".

**Always use a hyphen**  
-   Phrases that are listed in the dictionary with hyphens should always use them: "day-to-day variation", "one-to-one correspondence".

**Nouns**
-   Do not hyphenate a noun + gerund or fractions when they act as nouns in a sentence: "problem solving", "data taking", "one half", "two thirds", but do hyphenate them when they are used as an adjective, e.g. "data-taking period", "two-thirds full";
-   Use a hyphen with "self" compounds and a verb + preposition (acting as a noun): "self-destruct", "turn-on", "ramp-up".

**Words of equal weight**  
A hyphen is used to connect words of equal weight. Usually, they are connected because they have an "either-or", "from-to", or "between-and" relationship: "wave-particle duality", "argon-ethane gas", "time-space plot", "May-December relationship".

**American vs. British English**  
Note that British English has a tendency to hyphenate words that American English merges into one. This is particularly notable in the use of the prefix "non". For example, you should write "nonuniform", "nonprompt" and "nonperturbative" if your paper is written in American English but you can write "non-uniform", "non-prompt" and "non-perturbative" if you are, instead, using "British English". However, there are many exceptions to the British rule, such as "nonconformist" and "nonentity". It should also be noted that one can find the answer to many queries regarding hyphens by simply consulting a dictionary, such as Merriam–Webster (for American English) and OED (for British English). For example, we can find "on-board", "back-scatter", "back-end", "half-life" and "cut-off" in the OED. Another useful resource for hyphenation questions is the Chicago Manual of Style, which can be accessed through the CERN library proxy. The hyphenation guide is [here![](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/pub/TWiki/TWikiDocGraphics/external-link.gif)](https://www-chicagomanualofstyle-org.ezproxy.cern.ch/book/ed17/part2/ch07/psec089.html) and section 4 gives a list of exceptions and specific examples. Finally, it goes without saying that if "non" is followed by an acronym or a compound modifier, the hyphenation is always needed; e.g., non-QCD effects, non-Lorentz-invariant quantity (but, even better, we should avoid using such heavy constructs to start with).

**PACS keywords**  
When specifying the PACS keywords associated with a paper, hyphenate them as they appear in the [official listings![](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/pub/TWiki/TWikiDocGraphics/external-link.gif)](http://www.aip.org/publishing/pacs/pacs-2010-regular-edition).

\*En dashes and em dashes\*  
Use an en dash (a double hyphen "--" in LaTeX, with no spaces between it and the items on either side) to indicate a range of values, e.g. "5--10", or something attributed to two people, e.g. "Bose--Einstein correlations". Use an em dash (a triple hyphen "---" in LaTeX, with no spaces between it and the words on either side) for an article of punctuation, e.g. "The measurement failed---we messed it up." The em dash is also used to bring more emphasis to a comment than it would receive if placed in parentheses or in a clause set off by commas. For example, compare "Additional leptons, if present, result in a negligible increase in the background." with "Additional leptons---if present---result in a negligible increase in the background."

**Two related hyphenated words near each other**  
If you have two related hyphenated words near each other in a sentence, you can shorten the first hyphenated word by omitting the hyphenated part and leaving a hyphen at the end of it. Example: "Both initial- and final-state radiation are taken into account."

### Common grammatical mistakes
**Choice of indefinite article: "a" versus "an"**  
Use "a" before words that start with a consonant sound and "an" before words that start with a vowel sound, i.e., it is the sound that matters, not whether the word actually starts with a consonant or vowel. For example, we state "she has a one-track mind" because "one" starts with a "w" sound. Other examples: "an hour" \["o" sound\], "a historic" \["h" sound\], "an unfair judgement" \[vowel sound\], "a utopian dream" \["yoo" and thus a "y" sound\].

Concerning indefinite articles before an acronym, it depends whether we read the acronym as a word or spells out the letters, and on the corresponding vowel or consonant sound. Thus we would state "a SUSY prediction" because we pronounce "SUSY" as a word that begins with an "s" sound. However, we would write "an SM prediction" because we pronounce the letter S, which has a vowel sound "ess". (Note: you shouldn't expand the acronym and pronounce "standard model" when reading "SM", at least not from the point of view of choosing the article.)

Concerning the use or not of a definite article before some commonly used acronyms: we should write "at LEP", "at RHIC", "at SLAC", "at CERN", "at the SPS", "at the LHC", "at the Tevatron". Also, we can write "in CMS" or "in the CMS experiment" but we cannot write "in the CMS".

Never start a new sentence with an acronym. In particular, you need to write "The ATLAS Collaboration", "The LEP results".

**Allows, facilitates, permits**  
The phrases "allows to do something" and "allows to use" are incorrect. Alternative phrasings with the intended meaning are "allows something to be accomplished" or "allows the use of". There are verbs such as "facilitates" or "permits" that have a similar meaning to "allows" and for which the same grammatical rules apply.

**Due to**  
The phrase "due to" is commonly misused. It should only be used adjectivally and not adverbially. Thus: "The red sunset was due to dust in the atmosphere." is correct. But: "The sunset appeared red due to dust in the atmosphere." is not correct.

A simple test is to try replacing "due to" by "caused by". If the sentence still works, then your use of "due to" is correct. However, if replacing "due to" by "because of" works even better, then "due to" is not correct. Replace it with "because of".

**Evidence**  
The noun "evidence" takes no "s", even if meant in a plural sense. Similarly for "information", "importance", "performance" (as in "detector performance"), and "significance".

**Which vs. that: nonrestrictive and restrictive clauses**  
"Which" should be used for a nonrestrictive clause, i.e., a parenthetical comment that could be removed from the sentence without changing its meaning. A nonrestrictive clause is always offset in commas. In contrast, "that" should be used to introduce a restrictive clause, and there should be no comma(s). A restrictive clause qualifies the item discussed before it and thus changes the meaning of the sentence.

Nonrestrictive: "This procedure is effective at identifying the background, which arises from leptonic W boson decay." (All the background arises from leptonic W boson decay.)

Restrictive: "This procedure is effective at identifying the background that arises from leptonic W boson decay." (There are other sources of background than leptonic W boson decay; the procedure is aimed only at this latter source.)

Note that in common usage, "which" and "that" are often used interchangeably, but this is not correct in formal writing and we do not recommend this. Collins dictionary states: "The more formal the language, the more important it is to preserve the distinction between the two relative pronouns (referring back to "which" and "that"); but in informal or colloquial usage, the words are often used interchangeably." Using "which" in a restrictive clause can be confusing for grammatically cognizant readers because they expect "that" in such a clause and they may wonder whether a comma was inadvertently omitted, resulting in a different intended meaning. The APS journals (PRL, PRD, and PRC) will change the "which's" to "that's" in a restrictive clause, and vice-versa for a nonrestrictive clause, if they do not follow the rules stated above. The safest course of action is to always follow the above rules for "which" and "that".

**In general, avoid ending sentences with a preposition**  
However, in modern usage, this applies only to cases where an awkward sentence results. Collins Dictionary states: "The practice of ending a sentence with a preposition has been much condemned, but careful users avoid it only where it would be stylistically clumsy."

Thus, for example, write "This defines the sample from which the events were selected" rather than "This defines the sample which the events were selected from".

**Its vs. it's**  
"Its" without an apostrophe is the possessive of "it", e.g. "Its main feature is". "It's" with an apostrophe is the contraction for "it is" and should not be used in a CMS publication (see the section on contractions below).

**Associated to**  
"Associated to" is not a valid construction. Correct alternatives are "assigned" or "associated with". For example:  
"if a resulting track is found, it is assigned to the supercluster to form an electron candidate" (used to explain that an explicit action has been taken);  
"The uncertainty associated with the choice of the PDF in the simulation is evaluated" (used to describe the properties of an object).

## Numbers
Words should be used to indicate integer numbers up to 10, e.g., "there are four layers of chambers", except numerals should be used in the following circumstances:
-   When implying an arithmetical manipulation: "a factor of 7", "4 orders of magnitude", "5 times the height", "ratio of 3:5", "2 standard deviations";
-   With units of measure: "5 V", "10 s";
-   With divisions of the paper (part, paragraph, Section): "Section 3", or for numbered items (rule, model, equation): "model 1", "case 3";
-   When numbers are used as nouns: “values of 0 and 1".

However, given that a change in style at 10 is arbitrary, authors may prefer using numerals for all countable entities, except in idiomatic expressions: "Meyrin used to be a one-horse town, but with no horse."

If logic calls for a number to begin a sentence, title, or heading, then spell out the number. If possible, reword the sentence so that the number appears elsewhere.

When two numbers are adjacent, spell out the number more easily expressed in words and leave the other as a numeral, or reword the sentence to separate the numbers. In general, retain the numeral that occurs with a unit of measurement: change "Data were collected at eight 10 V increments" to "Data were collected at eight increments of 10 V each".

Make sure that a number and its units are not split across a line. The suggested units macros (including \\unit{...} itself) have a built-in thinspace, so if you write 5\\GeV, the resulting spacing is correct and LaTeX won't split the number and its unit across a line. This works both inside and outside mathmode.

Do not represent decimal points by commas: write 3.14 (not 3,14). To avoid ambiguity between European and American styles, write numbers with five or more digits on either side of the decimal point with spaces between each set of three numbers rather than commas (use a short space in LaTeX, "\\,") . For numbers with exactly four such digits, do not use a space or comma:
-   36\\,000, 6\\,385\\,321, 2048, 3.141\\,59.

However, if numbers with four digits on either side of the decimal point appear in a table, they should be punctuated with a small space as for numbers with five or more digits, if it is deemed to improve the aesthetics.

If you write a number in scientific notation, use the \\times symbol in LaTeX and not the \\cdot symbol. Example: $8 \\times 10^{-2}$. Do not use either the \\times or the \\cdot symbols in formulas where multiplication is the default operation, e.g. write “\\mathcal{B} \\sigma" for the product of cross section and branching fraction, and not “\\mathcal{B} \\times \\sigma" or “\\mathcal{B} \\cdot \\sigma". Do not use the symbol "\\sigma" to denote the product of cross section and branching fraction; use “\\mathcal{B} \\sigma".

If you are giving a number as a percentage with an uncertainty in the text, use parentheses around the number and its uncertainty. Example: "The muon efficiency is $(94.3 \\pm 1.3)\\%$."

If you are just giving a percentage, do not put a space between the number and the percentage sign, e.g. write "94.3\\%", not "94.3 \\%".

If you are giving a range of percentages, use "2--3%" not "2%--3%". Notice the use of the double hyphen to indicate a range, as discussed in the section on hyphens.

If you use expressions with "=", greater-than or less-than symbols, put the entire expression inside the mathmode environment to obtain the correct spacing, e.g. $\\pt > 200\\GeV$.

If there is no number to the left of the symbol, use curly braces around the symbol to obtain the correct spacing, e.g. "${>} 60\\GeV$", ${\\pm}3$", "${\\approx}4$". The curly braces let LaTeX know that the normally binary operator is spaced as a unary operator. This will leave the correct spacing between the operator and the number.

Only use the equal symbol "=" with integer numbers, not with real numbers. It is correct to write $10 < \\pt < 20\\GeV$, $\\abs{y} < 1.2$. It is incorrect to write "in two bins, $\\abs{y} < 0.6$ and $0.6 \\leq \\abs{y} < 1.2$". It is mathematically impossible that an event has a particle with \\abs{y} = 0.6; the rapidity being a real number, it will always be slightly larger or slightly smaller than 0.6 or any other specific number.

In ranges, do not apply the same number of digits unless they have a real meaning. Write, for instance, $0.9 < \\abs{y} < 1.25$ and not $0.90 < \\abs{y} < 1.25$. It does not make sense to write, for instance, $\\abs{y} < 1.50$, because the edges of the bins are decided by the analysts and, hence, have "infinite precision"; they are not the result of a measurement and there is no information content in the number of "zeros". One could just as well write "1.25000000000". It is correct (and important) to write 80.0\\invfb, for instance. It is useless and misleading to write "\\pt bins of edges $10.0, 12.5, 15.0, 17.5 and 20.0\\GeV$.

In the case of symmetric ranges, one should use the form $\\abs{y} < 1.2$, say, rather than $-1.2 < y < 1.2$, even in case there are other, nonsymmetric ranges also mentioned, such as: "The analysis is made in three bins: $-2.1 < y < -1.2$, $\\abs{y} < 1.2$, and $1.2 < y < 2.1$."

It is incorrect to write "ranging from 30--40%". It is also incorrect to write "between 30--40%". One should always use the formulations "ranging from 30 to 40%" or "in the 30--40% range" or "in the range between 30 and 40%".

See also [the section on commas](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/bin/view/CMS/Internal/PubGuidelines#Commas).

### Number of significant digits for measurements and uncertainties
-   In CMS papers, the standard is to use at most two significant digits for all quoted uncertainties, unless only one significant digit is appropriate.
-   The precision quoted for an uncertainty should match the precision quoted for the central value associated with it and the precision of any other associated quoted uncertainties.

Examples:
-   27.4 ± 0.1 (stat) ± 2.1 (syst): the measurement and the statistical uncertainty are given to only one digit after the decimal point to match the precision of the systematic uncertainty;
-   27.40 ± 0.14 (stat) ± 0.85 (syst): now the measurement and the statistical uncertainty are given to two digits after the decimal point to match the precision of the systematic uncertainty;
-   27.4 ± 1.3 (stat) ± 0.2 (syst): the systematic uncertainty is given with only 1 significant digit, to match the precision of the statistical uncertainty.

If an uncertainty is so small compared to the other uncertainties so that this guideline cannot be followed, do not quote the value of that uncertainty. Just say it is negligible compared to the other uncertainties. For example, if the statistical uncertainty in a measurement is ±0.001 and the systematic uncertainty is 2.2, we would quote the result as
-   27.4 ± 2.2 (syst), with a negligible statistical uncertainty.

Notice our convention for labeling the statistical and systematic uncertainties: "(stat)" and "(syst)", without a period at the end of each abbreviation and with a space before and after the parentheses.

See also [the previous section](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/bin/view/CMS/Internal/PubGuidelines#Numbers) for information on the number of digits that should be used in ranges.

## Acronyms
After publishing hundreds of papers, we feel that it is no longer necessary to define either the CMS or the LHC acronyms. However, the first time the LHC is mentioned in the text of a paper, it should be referred to as "the CERN LHC" or "the LHC at CERN". This should be done in the introduction. After that, you can refer to it simply as "the LHC". The abstract should state either the "LHC" or "CERN LHC" according to the author's taste.

We do not capitalize the first letters of words in a phrase that is about to be made into an acronym, e.g. "standard model (SM)", "quantum chromodynamics (QCD)", unless they are proper nouns like "Monte Carlo (MC) simulation program". Unless the meaning of an acronym is widely known outside the author's specialty (e.g. DNA), it should be defined the first time it is used in a document. For example, the acronym PDF is not widely known and can even be confusing; define it as "parton distribution function (PDF)" when first used. In general, periods are not used in acronyms ("CL" for "confidence level", but use the \\CL macro), but there are exceptions (e.g. "D.C." for "District of Columbia", as opposed to "DC" for "direct current").

Acronyms that are used quite often in CMS papers include: "two-dimensional (2D) fit", "confidence level (CL)", "level-1 (L1) trigger", "high-level trigger (HLT)", "tag-and-probe (T&P) method", "minimum-bias (MB) events", "parton distribution function (PDF)", "next-to-leading order (NLO)", "final-state radiation (FSR)".

We often use an acronym for "number of degrees of freedom". We suggest that you use either "dof" or "n\_d" (all in roman) for the acronym. Whatever you choose, do NOT use "d.o.f." or anything similar with periods in it. It is also more common to use "pdf" in lower case as the acronym for "probability density function".

The use of acronyms that refer to software packages without an explanation or reference is of little help to an outside reader. Define the acronym and/or give a reference for it. In referring to acronyms of external Monte Carlo programs such as GEANT, EVTGEN, MADGRAPH, etc., use the ptdr symbol macros, e.g. \\GEANTfour.

Do not start a sentence with an acronym (or a symbol).

Unless widely known, acronyms should not be used in the title of a manuscript:
-   "Measurement of triple gauge-boson couplings in proton-proton collisions at 13 TeV" (not "Measurement of TGCs in proton-proton collisions at 13 TeV").

An acronym (excluding MC names) should not be defined unless the associated phrase is used at least three times in the text. An acronym should be separately defined in the abstract and in the main body of the text, and should be defined in the abstract only if it will be used at least two more times in the abstract. Same thing for the summary: all used acronyms should be redefined, and introduced only if they will be used at least twice more. The acronym should always directly follow the definition, be placed inside parentheses, and may have an "s" added (for example "PDFs") to match the defining phrase. If the "s" is added in the definition, then the acronym without an "s" does not have to be defined also. Similarly, if the acronym without an "s" is defined, then the acronym with an "s" can be used without also defining it.
-   Physicists are very fond of three-letter acronyms (TLAs). A typical paper might have a new TLA in every paragraph.

The words in the definition should be capitalized only if they would otherwise be capitalized:
-   The Monte Carlo (MC) simulation of the cathode strip chambers (CSC) is implemented as described in Ref. \[23\].

## Symbols, MC parameters, units, and particle names
See Sections 6 and 7 of [Ref. \[1![](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/pub/TWiki/TWikiDocGraphics/external-link.gif)](https://gitlab.cern.ch/tdr/utils/raw/master/general/notes_for_authors.pdf)\] or the appendices of [Ref. \[2![](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/pub/TWiki/TWikiDocGraphics/external-link.gif)](https://gitlab.cern.ch/tdr/papers/XXX-08-000/raw/master/XXX-08-000_temp.pdf)\] below for a list of the CMS LaTeX symbol definitions and particle pennames. There is also a PDF attached to this twiki page ([macros.pdf](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/pub/CMS/Internal/PubGuidelines/macros.pdf)) which contains the full list of macros available.

Subscripts and superscripts that are abbreviations of English words, such as "T" for "transverse", must be written in roman font (in math mode, use "\\text" and not "\\mathrm"). Math indices (such as the i, j, k of matrix elements) must be written in italic (math mode). Use the standard CMS macros, when available. Make subscripts and superscripts succinct.

Particle names should always be written using the particle name macros (pennames), regardless of whether they appear in plain text or in expressions ("\\PW boson production", not "W boson production"; "$\\PH \\to \\PGm\\PGm$", not "$\\mathrm{H} \\to \\mu\\mu$", etc.) In general, use the penname macros beginning with \\P; the older CMS-style macros such as \\cPqb are now simply aliases to the \\P macros. A single penname does not need to be in math mode, as in the first example above, but if you have two or more pennames together then they should be in math mode to avoid extra spaces, so "$\\Pp\\Pp$ collisions", not "\\Pp\\Pp collisions".

`\DeclareRobustCommand{\PR}{{\HepParticle{R}{}{}}\Xspace}`  
Subscripts and superscripts go in the two sets of braces immediately after the `{R}`, respectively. Use `\HepAntiParticle`, `\HepSusyParticle`, or `\HepSusyAntiParticle` to add an overline, tilde, or both, respectively.

To refer to the anti-\\kt jet finder, use the CMS "\\kt" macro, i.e., "anti-\\kt". This yields an upper case roman subscript "$k\_{\\text{T}}$, consistent with CMS usage for other quantities, such as \\pt. We checked with the anti-\\kt jet finder authors that the upper case "T" subscript is perfectly fine with them, and noted that they themselves sometimes use the upper case T in their papers.

When referring to Monte Carlo parameters:  
if they are presented for their "meaning", they should be written in math mode, with subscripts and superscripts, such as $q\_\\mathrm{cut}$; if they are presented for its "code name", they should be shown as written in the code, in \\texttt, such as \\texttt{pThard}.

The standard CMS practice for a mass is to use lower case (italics) to indicate "mass" and a subscript with a penname to indicate the particle type, viz. $m\_{\\PW}$ for the W boson mass.

If a symbol appears immediately after the noun to which it refers, then a set of commas is not required around the symbol, e.g. "the transverse momentum \\pt of the muon". However, in cases like "the number of signal events, $N$, is evaluated" you should put commas, since $N$ doesn't come right after the noun "number" to which it refers.

Do not start a sentence with a symbol (or an acronym).

If a symbol is used in both the abstract and the body of the paper, it should be defined in both places.

Do not use the Greek letter σ to stand for standard deviation without defining it as such (and be careful you do not also use σ to mean a cross section). A recommended procedure is to use the words "standard deviation" everywhere in the text, viz., "2.3 standard deviations", and to not use the symbol σ. However, if the text then becomes tedious because of overuse of "standard deviation", including a definition of σ, as in "standard deviation (σ)", is recommended. You can use χ^2, with the Greek letter χ, to mean the chi-squared of a fit, without defining the symbol.

If you **do** introduce the symbol σ to mean "standard deviation", then do not use it to mean "cross section" in the same paper. Adding a subscript or using some other mechanism to differentiate between the two is allowed, viz., "σ<sub>exp</sub> or "σ<sub>theory</sub>" to designate an experimental or theoretical uncertainty in the legend of a plot, or using a subscript or superscript for the cross section version of "σ."

More generally, we should avoid as much as possible the use of the same typographical symbol to denote more than one entity. For example, it looks bad to see a sentence mentioning "the $\\eta$ distribution of the \\PGh meson" (where \\PGh is the \\eta particle name macro and, hence, is identical to $\\eta$). We know that some cases are unavoidable, such as the use of "y" for both the axis of a reference frame and for rapidity. Using $\\mu$ for the signal strength modifier (ratio between the measured cross sections or branching fractions and the corresponding SM expectations) is also a de facto standard, but it should be possible to compose sentences (and table headers) that do not mix this symbol with the identical Greek letter used to denote the muon particle.

The CMS x, y, z axes and the associated angles ϑ and φ no longer need to be defined. Instead, for example, at the end of the CMS detector description section, you could include the sentence: "A more detailed description of the CMS detector, together with a definition of the coordinate system used and the relevant kinematic variables, can be found in Ref.~\\cite{Chatrchyan:2008zzk}." See the [CMS detector description twiki page](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/bin/viewauth/CMS/Internal/PubDetector) for more details. The definitions of the pseudorapidity η and the total transverse momentum \\pt also no longer need to be given in a paper, since they are found in the reference given above.

The values of the angles ϑ and φ are implicitly assumed to be expressed in radians and that information does not need to be given in the paper. In particular, in expressions such as "\$\Delta R = \sqrt{\smash\[b\]{\Delta\eta\\,^2 + \Delta\phi\\,^2}}$", there is no need to state that \\phi is in radians. In general, for other angular quantities as well, there is no need to specify the units if those units are radians. If, exceptionally, an angle is given in degrees, it should be stated, using the symbol ^\\circ.

The axis labels "x", "y", and "z" should be written in math mode. Note that "$x$ axis" should not be hyphenated, and you should write "$x$-$y$ plane".

The rapidity parameter y does not need to have a defining equation in a paper. It is a well established variable.

Use the calligraphic "B" in LaTeX $\mathcal{B}$ to denote a branching fraction.

Missing transverse momentum should be defined. An example sentence for how to do this is "The missing transverse momentum vector \\ptvecmiss is defined as the projection onto the plane perpendicular to the beam axis of the negative vector sum of the momenta of all reconstructed particle-flow objects in an event. Its magnitude is referred to as \\ptmiss."

Here are a few more words (from published CMS papers) that might be useful guidance: "The energy scale corrections applied to jets are propagated to ptmiss. We define HT as the scalar pT sum of all reconstructed jets in the event that have pT > 30 [GeV](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/bin/edit/CMS/Internal/GeV?topicparent=CMS/Internal.PubGuidelines;nowysiwyg=1 "this topic does not yet exist; you can create it.") and |eta| < 2.4. In addition, we define the ST as the scalar sum of ptmiss, the pT of leptons, and the HT in the event."

Note that the PubComm recommends using the symbol \\ptmiss ( = p\_T^{miss}) to represent the magnitude of the missing transverse momentum vector \\ptvecmiss, for consistency of notation. Also, use of the expression "missing transverse energy" is strongly discouraged. Please use "missing transverse momentum" because the physical quantity is a vector, not a scalar. Same thing for "transverse energy": use "transverse momentum" instead. The fact that ET was used in earlier CMS papers does not justify its continued use. Finally, a pT (or ET) with a slash through it is strongly discouraged as a substitute for the symbol \\ptmiss, because the "slashed" symbols are visually very similar to the corresponding unslashed quantities (making them less readily distinguishable from their opposites), are less self explanatory, and sometimes do not print properly.

You can use the LaTeX symbol $\\approx$ in a sentence as a substitute for the word "approximately", e.g. "the systematic uncertainty is ${\\approx}3\\%$. Do not leave a space between the symbol and the number. The ${\\approx}$ macro will put in the correct spacing.

Use the LaTeX symbol $\\sim$ (~) to mean "goes as", i.e., when you give the functional form of something without giving the normalization.

Do not write fractions inline using the $\\frac$ LaTeX command. Use the "/" symbol instead.

Symbols for physical quantities, such as "c" for the speed of light or "e" for the electron charge, should be written in italics (math mode in LaTeX), while symbols for units are written in roman type. For instance, it is wrong to write "10 _mm_". Use "10\\unit{mm}" instead.

We strongly recommend the use of the LaTeX symbols \\GeV, \\GeVc, and \\GeVcc defined in the CMS style file for producing the corresponding names. The symbols can be used both inside and outside of mathmode: e.g. $2\\GeV$ or 2\\GeV, and the appropriate spacing between the number and unit is then already included. If you want units for a table heading or a figure label that do not have a number with them, use the penname macros such as \\GeVns, which do not put a space before the unit.

The energy of a particle is given in GeV (remember, use the macro "\\GeV"). Its mass and momentum can also be given in GeV (natural units, ħ=c=1) or, alternatively, as GeV/$c^2$ and GeV/$c$, respectively (use the macros "\\GeVcc" and "\\GeVc"). The PubComm strongly recommends the use of natural units, but authors may select the other convention should they so desire. However, the selection should be consistent throughout a given paper, including the figures (axis labels, legends). If you do not use natural units, then be aware that rapidity should be written as:

y = \\frac{1}{2} \\ln \[\\frac{E + cp\_z}{E - cp\_z}\],

where _c_ is a physical constant, not a unit. Note also that the electron selection variable, which is commonly written as _E/p_, should be _E/pc_ if natural units are not used, since it is dimensionless.

A derived unit that is formed by the division of two others may be written as either m/s$^2$ or as m s$^{-2}$. However, in complicated cases, negative exponents should be used: m kg s$^{-3}$ A$^{-1}$, not m kg/s$^3$/A. Units of instantaneous luminosity should be written as cm$^{-2}s$^{-1} and not as 1/cm$^{2}$/s \[normally the macro \\percms should be used\]. Similarly, units of integrated luminosity should be written as fb$^{-1}$ (macro \\fbinv), and not 1/fb.

Add the word "events" or "candidates" as the unit in cases like "The background is 4.1 ± 1.3 events". Only write the unit once when giving a range of values: "We require the mass to satisfy 5.23 < m < 5.33\\GeV".

Acronyms (e.g. SUSY), and superscripts and subscripts (e.g. vis, miss, and jet) should be printed in roman type. In CMS LaTeX: (a) mathematical functions should use their TeX names, e.g. \\sin, \\exp, \\log; (b) when used as tags for other quantities, words and word fragments should be represented using, e.g. \\text{jet}.

The ECAL shower shape variables σ\_{iηiη} and σ\_{ηη} were invented to distinguish photons from background in the ECAL. In practice, only the latter variable is used in analyses and described in papers. Therefore, the PubComm and ECAL communities decided not to use the name σ\_{iηiη} in papers, but instead to call that variable σ\_{ηη} in our publications.

Temperatures given in Celsius should have the degree symbol (\$^\\circ\$C), but not if given in Kelvin (K).

We should either write "center-of-mass energy" or "sqrt(s)", but not both. The symbol is a well established variable and does not need to be defined.

## Word usage and jargon

**Actual**  
Be careful using the English word "actual", which is a semi-false cognate to the French "actuel" and the German "aktuell", meaning current, existing, or present-day. The English word has the additional meanings of real, genuine, or true. So a phrase such as "the actual setup" implies that there may be a fake setup somewhere; so write "the current (or existing) setup" instead.

**Adjectives modifying agreement**  
In describing the level of agreement between two distributions or results, authors are encouraged to not be excessive in their characterization of the agreement. Phrases like "are in excellent agreement" or "are in very good agreement" can appear overly exuberant and inappropriately dispassionate for a scientific paper. They are usually better expressed by the more emotionally neutral "are in agreement". If some level of fervor is deemed essential, authors could consider the phrase "good agreement". However, the use of "very" is strongly discouraged.

Similarly, the phrase "are in very poor agreement" could be replaced by the dispassionate "are in disagreement" and have greater effect because of its larger sense of objectiveness.

If the exact level of agreement is deemed to be important, the authors are encouraged to give some quantitative value (chi-squared, number of standard deviations apart, Kolmogorov-Smirnov test result, etc.) to indicate how well the two things agree.

**Antiquarks**  
Do not use terms like "antitop quark". Use "top antiquark" instead. Similarly, use "top quark and antiquark" when referring to both types.

**Beamspot**  
This term is considered to be jargon and should not be used in a paper unless it is first specifically defined in the text. If you mean "interaction point", "beam axis", or "luminous region", then it is better to use one of these terms instead.

**Charged tracks**  
Tracks are not charged, particles are. Use "charged particle tracks" instead of "charged tracks". Only use "track" when referring to a charged, as opposed to a neutral, reconstructed object. Distinguish clearly between "particles" that are the underlying physical objects, and "tracks" (or "clusters") that are the things we measure.

**Collaboration**  
We capitalize "collaboration" in phrases like "the CMS Collaboration" or "the ATLAS and CMS Collaborations" because in this context it is a proper noun, i.e., the formal name of the experiment(s), but not in expressions like "the Tevatron collaborations" where it is not a proper noun because it is not the name of an experiment. Write "the D0 Collaboration" without the fancy symbol for zero. Where suitable, use the following bibtex record or analogous:  
author = "{ATLAS and CMS Collaborations, and LHC Higgs Combination Group}",

**Colloquial expressions**  
Avoid colloquial usage common in spoken English but improper in formal writing. For example, instead of "We get rid of background events by requiring" one could write "We remove background events by requiring"

**Convolved not convoluted**  
The correct verb to go with the noun "convolution" is "convolved", not "convoluted", which means something entirely different. Example: "a Breit-Wigner function convolved with a Gaussian resolution function".

**Data**  
The word "data" is plural. So the phrase "the data are" is correct, while "the data is" is incorrect. Think of the phrase "data points" when you write "data". Use the singular expression "data sample" or "data set" if one of them is better suited to your aim. Avoid the seldom-used singular term for data, which is "datum".

**Data set** vs. **Dataset**  
We can use one form or the other but, of course, not both in the same paper; consistency within each paper is very important.

**Discriminant vs. discriminator**  
While both words mean "a distinguishing characteristic", “discriminant” is more commonly used in a scientific or mathematical context, and “discriminator” is used in more general parlance. Thus, we should definitely use the former in CMS publications.

**Fake**  
"Fake" is an example of physics slang. Use "hadron misidentified as a lepton" \[or similar\] instead of "fake lepton", and "misidentification rate" instead of "fake rate". (Note that if you merely state "misidentified lepton", it can be ambiguous whether you mean that an object, such as a hadron, is erroneously identified as a lepton, or rather that a genuine lepton is misidentified as something else.) Sometimes the "fake leptons" are real leptons, just not from the signal source being targeted. In this case, you might use "nonprompt leptons" or some other explicit explanation. In the end, it will depend on what you intend to distinguish between the different types of particles identified as leptons. But do not use "fake".

**Higgs boson**  
The term "Higgs boson" should be used throughout the text of CMS papers making reference to this particle. "Higgs mass" is what the Scottish theorist would read off his bathroom scale; use "Higgs boson mass". Similarly, use "Higgs boson production cross section", "Higgs boson decays", etc. If you want to define a symbol for the standard model Higgs boson, use "H". At the first occurrence, the symbol H should be introduced thus : "The Higgs boson (H) ...". The symbol H can then be used to represent the Higgs boson in formulae (and possibly in figures and tables).

**Kinematics**  
Avoid using "kinematics" as a noun unless you are referring to the field of kinematics. Rather than "The kinematics were varied" write "The kinematical variables were varied".

**Monte Carlo**  
The term "Monte Carlo" refers to a simulation technique. Therefore, although "Monte Carlo program" makes perfect sense, it generally would be better to use "simulated events" rather than "Monte Carlo events". Do not use "Monte Carlo" as a noun. It is an adjective. Do not write "We tuned the Monte Carlo", but rather "We tuned the Monte Carlo program".

**Particle names as nouns**  
Avoid using particle names as nouns, i.e., write "W boson decay" (with pennames, "{\\PW} boson decay") and not "W decay", "J/\\psi meson production" and not "J/\\psi production". However, this rule can be relaxed if the text becomes tedious.

**Physics jargon**  
Avoid using physics slang such as "cuts", "cut tuning", etc. Use "require" instead of "cut on", "selection criteria" instead of "cuts", etc.

**Pileup**  
This is an example of physics jargon. If you want to use the word "pileup", you should define it the first time it appears in the text.

**QCD events, QCD jets**  
Most events at the LHC are produced through gluon fusion and could thus be called "QCD events" regardless of the presence of a top quark-antiquark pair, or of a W, Z, or Higgs boson. Thus, the expression "QCD events" or "QCD jets" without a definition is rather meaningless. Same thing for an expression like "multijet events". For example, a "multijet event" could also be a "Z+jets event" in which the Z boson decays hadronically. Thus define what you mean on first occurrence, e.g., "the background from SM events composed uniquely of jets produced through the strong interaction, referred to as quantum chromodynamics (QCD) multijet events." That may seem complicated, but in fact the situation is complicated. Following such a definition, you could then merely use "multijet events" or "QCD events" or "QCD multijet events."

As for all acronyms (with exceptions like "CMS" and "LHC", see above), define "QCD" on first occurrence as in the above example.

**Run 1 and Run 2**  
Do not use the term "Run 1" or "Run 2" in the title or abstract of a CMS physics paper because it is LHC jargon whose meaning will be lost to subsequent generations and which has no significance outside our field. It is OK to introduce the terms in the body of a paper, so long as it is clearly defined which data sets are implied.

**Sensible/sensitive**  
The French word "sensible" means "sensitive" in English; write "sensitive volume" and not "sensible volume".

**Statistics**  
Do not use the term "statistics" as a substitute for "amount of data". Write "the larger sample of events" rather than "the higher statistics", and "improve the statistical precision" rather than "improve the statistics".

**Supersymmetric particle names**  
Use "top squark" to designate the SUSY partner of the top quark, and similarly for the other SUSY squarks. Do not use "stop" or "sbottom". The corresponding terms for the supersymmetric leptons are trickier because phrases like "electron slepton" are quite awkward. We advocate defining terms such as "stau", and "selectron" in the text, and then using them throughout the rest of the paper. As an example, SUS-13-002 contains the following statement: "A gluino-initiated cascade can also yield a multilepton state dominated by τ leptons should the superpartner of the τ lepton (stau) be substantially lighter than the partners of the electron and muon (selectron and smuon, respectively) ..."

**Systematics**  
"Systematics" as a noun refers to a field of biology. Use "systematic uncertainty" or "systematic bias" instead.

**Tau lepton**  
One of three different forms are recommended when referring to a tau lepton:

-   In a sentence where \\Pe and \\Pgm are used, \\Pgt should be used, where \\Pe, \\Pgm, and \\Pgt are the pennames for the electron, muon, and tau lepton, respectively;

-   In a sentence where electron and/or muon are spelled out, "tau lepton" should be spelled out; do not refer to "the tau" or to "taus" but rather to "the tau lepton" or "tau leptons", as in "electrons, muons and tau leptons were selected";

-   In sentences where the tau lepton alone is mentioned, use "tau lepton" or, if the meaning is clear, simply "\\Pgt".

**Top quark tagging and Higgs boson tagging**  
The PubComm discourages the use of terms such as "top tagging" and "Higgs tagging" as shortened forms of "top quark tagging" and "Higgs boson tagging" since they constitute jargon. Using "top" in place of "top quark" is incorrect because "top" is not a noun but a modifier of the word "quark". Similarly, "Higgs" is a modifier of the word "boson". Shortened formed such as "t tagging" and "H tagging" are acceptable provided that a proper definition of these terms is given at their first occurrence.

**Uncertainty in vs. uncertainty on**  
While both the phrases "uncertainty in" and "uncertainty on" have been used in HEP papers, the Oxford English Dictionary is clear that "uncertainty in" is the correct usage. We strongly encourage the use of this form and discourage "uncertainty on".

**Uncertainty vs. error**  
An "error" is a mistake. So do not use this term when you mean "uncertainty". Thus, for example, write "statistical uncertainty", "uncertainty propagation", "relative uncertainty", etc. Use "convariance matrix" instead of "error matrix". However, it is acceptable to refer to "error bars" when referring to the uncertainties on the points of a plot (alternatively, these could be called "vertical bars"). If you have a plot in which the points have both horizontal and vertical bars, with the horizontal bars representing the bin width, then you should state that the vertical bars show the uncertainties and the horizontal bars the bin widths (note: horizontal bars should be used only if the bin widths in the plot vary).

**Refer to your result as a concrete observable**  
Do not use expressions like "Measurements of \\ttbar production are obtained..." or "Limits on the pair production of squarks and gluinos are derived..." because "production" is not an observable. Instead, refer to a concrete observable such as a cross section, a polarization, etc. If it is appropriate to be more generic, you could refer to "properties." Thus, it would be correct to state "Measurements of \\ttbar production properties are obtained ..." or "Limits on the cross sections for the pair production of gluinos and squarks are derived..."

## Contractions
Do not use contractions, e.g. let's, don't, can't, won't, it's., shan't.

## Nested parentheses
The APS journals Phys. Rev. Lett. and Phys. Rev. D do not allow "nested parentheses" in their papers. These are parentheses that are inside another set of parentheses, such as:  
"(2.1 +\\- 0.3 (stat) +\\- 0.2 (syst)) x 10^-3" and "The theoretical prediction (Eq. (2)) is compared..."

In such cases, the outside parentheses must be replaced with square brackets, as specified by the APS Style Guide. The above examples should become:  
"\[2.1 +\\- 0.3 (stat) +\\- 0.2 (syst)\] x 10^-3" and "The theoretical prediction \[Eq. (2)\] is compared..."

## Miscellaneous
-   Do not capitalize the phrase "standard model" since it is not a proper noun.
-   Words made from proper nouns should be capitalized, e.g. "Gaussian" and "Lagrangian". Exceptions are "fermion" and "boson".
-   Common particle names should be spelled out, e.g., "muon" and "electron", when used in a sentence. For a generic charged lepton, use "lepton" in a sentence and the symbol \\Pell in equations, but if this means electrons and muons only (not tau leptons), make this clear.
-   The full quark name should be used in most cases, e.g. "charm quark" instead of just "charm", "charm quark mass" instead of "charm mass". However, they can be shortened to "c quark" and "c quark mass".
-   Do not write "cross section times branching fraction" but rather "the product of the cross section and branching fraction" (and use the symbols “\\mathcal{B} \\sigma").
-   Do not write "a 95% CL". There is only one CL of 95%, thus if an article is used, it should be "the 95% CL". However, for most occurrences, "95% CL", with no article, is recommended.
-   In the text, write "Figure 3" when starting a new sentence and "Fig. 3" elsewhere. Similarly, use "Equation (1)" at the start of a sentence, but "Eq. (1)" elsewhere. Write such phrases as "Table 3", "Section 3", and "Appendix 1" both at the start of a sentence and elsewhere. For plurals, use "Figs. 2 and 3", "Eqs. (2) and (3)", "Tables 1 and 2", "Sections 3 and 4", and "Appendices 1 and 2". In referring to a certain plot in a figure, use "as shown in Fig. 1 (left)" or "as shown in Fig. 1 (a)". However, if a specific table, figure, section, or equation number is not given, you should not use a capital letter, e.g. "the uncertainties given in the tables", "all figures are normalized", "in the following section". Do not let LaTeX separate one of these from its associated number across a line break. Use a "~" before the number to prevent this, e.g. "Eq.~(2)". Similarly, do not let reference numbers get separated across a line break. Always put a "~" before the reference number, e.g. "The CMS detector is described elsewhere~\[1\]."
-   In mentioning Monte Carlo generators such as MadGraph+Pythia in a paper. a good descriptive phrase of their characteristics is: "a fixed-order perturbative QCD calculation of up to four noncollinear high-\\pt partons, supplemented with parton showering and multiparton interactions.”

## Guidelines for the format of CMS publications
In this part of the guidelines, we discuss the standards for formatting a document in the CMS style. These guidelines should be used as the basis for all internal reviews of paper drafts and for any paper posted to the arXiv. More technical information and examples can be found in the references given below.

## Title
The title should be clear and concise. Where possible, use words rather than symbols unless this would compromise clarity. Do not use undefined symbols like \\ptmiss but rather write out "missing transverse momentum". The first word of the title is always capitalized, but the following words should be in lower case unless they are capitalized in normal use. It is our convention to not mention the CMS experiment in the title of physics papers, since the author is listed as "The CMS Collaboration" directly below the title. For papers about the detector or detector performance, it is acceptable to mention "CMS" in the title. Similarly, we normally do not put "LHC" in a title. We have also decided not to use "Run 1" or "Run 2" in a title and do not normally list the integrated luminosity in a title.

## Abstract
Unlike most of the abstracts submitted to conferences, where the final results are often not disclosed, the abstracts of our papers should be a self-contained summary of the paper, ending with the main results of the analysis, expressed through clear sentences and, whenever possible, quantitative information. In particular, the following information should be mentioned: experiment name, accelerator name, type of beams, center-of-mass energy, and integrated luminosity. All symbols need to be introduced, including transverse momentum. At the same time, it is important to keep the text as succinct and impactful as possible: a short and crisp abstract attracts the attention of the potential reader, who can then find all the details in the paper itself, while exhaustive descriptions tend to push people away. In any case, the abstracts of CMS papers cannot exceed 200 words, which is a limit imposed by many of the journals we publish in (violations of this rule will lead to easily-avoidable delays at the submission step). In the case or PRL, the limit is 600 characters, which means around 100 words.

It might be worth noting the following abstract-length limits, specific to the arXiv and several journals:

| [Journal](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/bin/view/CMS/Internal/PubGuidelines?sortcol=0;table=5;up=0#sorted_table "Sort by this column") | [abstract length limit](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/bin/view/CMS/Internal/PubGuidelines?sortcol=1;table=5;up=0#sorted_table "Sort by this column") |
| --- | --- |
| PRD | About 5% of article length and less than 500 words |
| PRL | 600 characters, which means 100 words at 6 characters/word |
| EPJC | 200 words |
| arXiv | 1920 characters, which is around 320 words at 6 characters/word |
| PLB | "concise and factual" |
| JHEP | "must fit in the first page" |

## PDF author
The PDFAuthor line should be a list of the names of the authors of the paper, with the contact author's name first. Do not put "CMS Collaboration".

## PDF title
The PDFTitle line should be an exact copy of the real title, except remove any LaTeX symbols, e.g. change $\\sqrt{s}$ to sqrt(s), $B^+$ to B+, $\\chi\_{c1}$ to chi(c1), $\\bar{b}$ to b-bar. See the titles and abstracts of our papers on the arXiv site for examples of this procedure.

## Section headings
The number of section levels should generally be limited to 4. The headings in a short note do not need to be numbered. For a longer paper with many sections, use LaTeX-style numbering. Capitalize only the first word in headings and subheadings, except for words that would normally be capitalized or acronyms. Do not start headings or subheadings with articles such as "The", "A", or "An". However, if the next word in the heading is an acronym or mathematical symbol, then start the heading with "The". Thus, the title for the detector description section should be "The CMS detector". Do not title the final section of the text as "Conclusions" unless you present a discussion of results that is not already in the main body of the paper. Otherwise, label it as "Summary".

## Figures and tables
A standard format style for CMS plots has been approved by the PubComm Steering Board. The primary goal is to display the CMS name in a visible way, so that it appears at first glance, and to present the integrated luminosity and center-of-mass energy is a simple, compact manner. Please see [https://cms-analysis.docs.cern.ch/guidelines/plotting/![](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/pub/TWiki/TWikiDocGraphics/external-link.gif)](https://cms-analysis.docs.cern.ch/guidelines/plotting/) for details. (Older recommendations, now superseded, can be found at [https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/bin/view/CMS/Internal/FigGuidelines](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/bin/view/CMS/Internal/FigGuidelines).) The label "Preliminary" should be removed from the figures in the paper draft that goes to Collaboration Wide Review, so that the figures appear as intended for submission to a journal; it should be kept in the PAS version, as all results remain "preliminary" until the paper is submitted for publication. All figures showing data results should give the center-of-mass energy and the integrated luminosity corresponding to the results being presented.

To produce figures, use the TDR ROOT and style macros, or the Python equivalents, described in Ref. \[2\] and at [https://cms-analysis.docs.cern.ch/guidelines/plotting/![](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/pub/TWiki/TWikiDocGraphics/external-link.gif)](https://cms-analysis.docs.cern.ch/guidelines/plotting/). We strongly recommend that authors gather (in GitLab) all the files (macros, dot-C files or canvas files) necessary to reproduce all the figures in a paper.

Figures and tables should be placed in the text as close as possible to the page on which they are first mentioned. But they should not appear before that page.

All figures and tables should have captions. Figure captions should appear below the figure and table captions above the table. Table captions should use the \\topcaption command. If a table has footnotes, they should appear just below the table. In general, there should not be information in a figure or table caption that is not also in the text, but descriptive information about curves and points in a plot can be included in a caption but not the text. Descriptions of the analysis should be in the text and not in a caption. It is acceptable to state "\[as\] described in the text" in the caption to avoid duplication for some issues.

Be sure to label both axes of a plot and include the appropriate units for both. If you put scales such as "x 10\*\*-3" on the x or y axis of a figure, make sure they do not extend beyond the figure box. If they do, this makes rescaling the figure size difficult during journal formatting.

For one-dimensional plots showing the number of events per bin using a fixed bin width, give the binning in the y-axis label, e.g. "Events / 50 MeV" or "Events / 0.1 units" (if the x axis is dimensionless).

Horizontal bars on data points in one-dimensional plots should only be used to indicate the binning if the bin width varies across the plot. In that case, the meaning of the horizontal bars should be clearly explained in the accompanying figure caption.

For one-dimensional plots with variable bin width, a choice may be made to rescale the y axis to display the number of events per bin, divided by the bin width. In that case, for every bin, the number of entries should be divided by the bin width, and the y-axis label should read "< Events / \[unit\] >" (e.g. "< Events / GeV >"), where \[unit\] matches the scale used for the x axis. (Note the difference in the meanings of the labels "Events / 1.0 GeV" and "< Events / GeV >".) The brackets signify that what is being plotted is an average over an interval in which the event frequency may have changed considerably. Again, this should be clearly explained in the accompanying figure caption. If the choice is not to rescale the number of events by the bin width, the y-axis label should read "Events / bin".

In the legend of a plot, a symbol representing a data point should carry the same exact bars as in the plot itself, that is vertical only for fixed binning and both horizontal and vertical for varying binning.

The units in the axis labels for plots or in the column or row labels for tables can be put in either parentheses, e.g. "\\pt (GeV)", or brackets, e.g. "\\pt \[GeV\]".

If you use ROOT to make your figures and have a minus sign as a superscript in your legend or axis labels, you will notice that ROOT uses a hyphen for the minus sign, which does not look similar to what is done for a plus sign in a superscript. This will be noticeable if you have something like mu^+mu^- in your figure. To fix this, replace ^- with ^{#font\[122\]{\\55}} in your ROOT command.

To approximate the Latex symbol \\mathcal{B} for a branching fraction in ROOT, we recommend #bf{#it{#Beta}}

In case of varying bin size across a histogram, the data points should be correctly located horizontally. See the recommendations by the Statistics Committee at: [https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/bin/view/CMS/StatComWideBins](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/bin/view/CMS/StatComWideBins) and the link to "where to stick your data points" therein.

In plotting error bars for histograms with small numbers of events per bin, please adhere to the following advice of the CMS Statistics Committee:

The Statistics Committee invites you to use asymmetric error bars with correct coverage for event counts with Poisson variates, when the number of entries in a significant portion of the spectrum are small. This may either arise in low-statistics histograms, or in high-statistics ones drawn on a semi-logarithmic scale. These "correct coverage" error bars, first derived by Garwood, are obtained from the Neyman construction using the central interval convention for N>0, and drawing the upper limit for N=0.” See [https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/bin/viewauth/CMS/PoissonErrorBars](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/bin/viewauth/CMS/PoissonErrorBars) for an example of how to implement this procedure.

Use of colour in figures is encouraged, but the figures should also be intelligible in black and white. We supply colour figures for online posting of our papers, but we do not pay for having them printed in colour, so they are converted to black and white for the printed versions. Thus, use different line styles (hatching, dotted lines, etc.) and symbols (circles, squares, etc.) to distinguish various elements, in addition to colour. The caption can refer to colour in the figure, if that improves the clarity of the text. Use large enough labels, characters, symbols, and curve thicknesses for easy viewing. Characters should preferably be no less than a few point sizes smaller than the body text.

With the possible exception of exceedingly dense scatter plots or screen grabs, figures should NOT use bit-mapped graphics but instead use vector graphics (e.g., pdf files). This means figures should NOT be produced by plain-drawing packages such as Powerpoint.

The use of subfigure identifiers \[(a), (b), etc.\] in figures is allowed only if they are embedded into the pdf of the subplots themselves. Do NOT use plain-TeX positioning commands or the subfig/subfigure LaTeX commands to place the subfigure identifiers on or below a figure (this applies to both PAPERs and PASes). The CMS Publications Office must remove this command from the LaTeX file before the paper is submitted, which results in a delay. Use the style macros to put in the identifiers instead. Many people refer to subplots as "left", "right", "upper right", etc., to avoid complications with the "(a)", "(b)" formatting. If the paper discusses top and/or bottom quarks, squarks, etc., the "upper" and "lower" designations are preferred to "top" and "bottom" when referring to the locations of subplots, to avoid possible confusion. An advantage of NOT embedding "(a)", "(b)" into the subplots, and instead using the "(left)", "(right)", "(upper)", "(lower)" in the text and captions, is that the plot will then be more clean when lifted to show in a talk or include in a review article, etc.

Figures should be produced in .pdf format (since some journals only accept this), but it may be wise to produce .png format too, if you will put the figures on a public CMS twiki.

Sizing figures appropriately: In general, do NOT use the old-style, full-page format for figures. When these figures are resized down to a column width, the labels often become too small to read. It is better to have the figures near their design size so that any such problems are obvious in the CMS preprint format. Figures can be made modestly larger than they will appear in the journal, but a full-page figure should be important enough to have a large percentage of a journal page devoted to it, because that's what will end up happening.

All journals to which we submit, except for JHEP, and JINST, have a two-column-wide format. This means that all tables and figures must be sized to be either one column or two columns wide. The problems occur when the content or the labels in the plots do not scale well to the single-column width, but yet there's not enough content or space to justify making them two columns wide. This is particularly a problem for PRL, where the space limit is usually a critical concern. Try to keep all of your figures to either of the two standard sizes, unless there is some overriding motivation to do otherwise.

If you have a figure with two panels, with identical horizontal axes, you can save space by placing the two panels on the top of each other, without an intermediate gap, if they fit in a single column. But remember that the same figure also needs to be used in the preprint (arXiv) version, even if there we do not have space limitations.

The tables can actually be more of a problem than the figures. Many tables are three quarters of a page wide for no good reason. They end up having to be formatted two columns wide, and for PRL this is a serious drawback. Unnecessary blank space in tables should be eliminated, and very wide tables should be avoided, if possible.

Try to minimize the number of vertical and horizontal divider lines that you use in tables. They can make a table look unnecessarily cluttered. See the format used in PRL articles as a guide. Their guidelines say: "Use a single horizontal rule to separate headings and entries. Use horizontally running space to distinguish broad groups among entries, and extra vertically running space for columnar groups."

## Footnotes
The use of footnotes in CMS papers is only allowed in very exceptional cases. While one might tend to think that some "details" are better left out of the main text and "only" mentioned in a footnote, the net effect is exactly the opposite. The typical reader will be immediately attracted to the footnotes, which "jump into our eyes". It is easy to skip portions of the main text but one cannot avoid reading the footnotes: they stand out of the text and, hence, get a huge visibility. The information can usually be incorporated parenthetically into the text, with a minimal break to the natural flow of the narrative, or left out altogether. Also, do not replace footnotes by "endnotes"; notes in references are also discouraged and can only be used for additional information regarding the reference, e.g. "See also...".

Very exceptionally, the very high visibility of a footnote might be justified. Here is an example of such an exceptional case: we submit a paper and between the submission date and the publication date something important happens, that needs to be added to the paper. It doesn't fit in the flow of the text (and maybe the paper was already accepted), so that a footnote might be the best option. For instance: "After submission of this manuscript, the ATLAS collaboration made public a measurement of the same observable, the two results being compatible." Such footnotes must be kept as succinct as possible and should remain very exceptional, typically when we get to know something only once our own paper is already accepted.

In any case, "details" are surely not worth the exceptionally high visibility of a footnote.

## Acknowledgements
All CMS papers submitted for publication should have an appropriate Acknowledgements section. See [Acknowledgements to include in CMS Scientific Publications](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/bin/viewauth/CMS/Internal/PubAcknow) for the exact wording to use. If you are submitting the paper to an American (APS) journal, then you will need to change "centres" to "centers" in the phrase "we greatly acknowledge the computing centres", and change "programme to "program" in "Marie-Curie programme" and "HOMING PLUS programme". **Since the most recent version of the acknowledgements is always used by George at the time of submission, we no longer ask the acknowledgements to be included in CWR/FR, or post-FR versions of the papers.**

## References
A public CMS note or paper cannot cite an internal CMS document or a conference talk. See the guide on [which documents can be cited](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/bin/viewauth/CMS/Internal/WhatCanBeQuoted). Do not cite references that are unlikely to be available to the average reader. Do not blindly copy references from other papers without checking that the page and volume numbers are correct. Cite journal papers, if available, rather than a preprint. Do not cite a webpage as there is no guarantee it will be maintained and be available in the future. CMS documents should be cited with "CMS Collaboration" as the author until the paper is published. Thereafter, the first author, as spelled by the journal, plus "and others", should be listed as the author. In CMS, the format of the first author is always suppressed in favor of the collaboration name. The same applies to papers from other large collaborations (CDF, D0, ATLAS, OPAL, etc).

References should be numbered consecutively in the order in which they are cited in the text. The general guidelines for choosing references are:
1.  Reference all papers that were useful in preparing and making the specific measurement.
2.  Reference extensively previous measurements (in particular from other LHC and Tevatron experiments).
3.  Reference relevant theoretical papers, especially ones that predict a value for your measurement.
4.  Give a minimal general bibliography for the nonexpert reader who might need to be educated on the subject. These are papers typically referred to in the introduction.

When preparing the BibTeX bibliography, remember the following:
1.  You should check all the information in each reference. Do not assume that just because you have copied it from another paper it is correct. Click on the DOI link and make sure the correct article comes up. Then check the authors' names, the title, and the journal reference.
2.  Every journal citation should include its DOI.
3.  As a courtesy to readers who might not have access to a particular journal, include the arXiv number of all references that have them in the BibTeX listing. Many journals will not publish this number, but it will be shown in our preprints.
4.  All URLs for CMS notes, ANs, etc, should refer to those on the CDS server, not on the CMS server.
5.  CMS Notes that are not available on CDS are regarded as private and cannot be cited in a CMS publication.
6.  If a collaboration is referenced by the collaboration name and not by a first author, then the collaboration name should be listed in the AUTHOR field in the BibTeX file, e.g. author = "{CMS Collaboration}", If it is referenced by a first author plus "and others", the collaboration name must be listed in the COLLABORATION field. In CMS, we use the collaboration name in preference to the first author, but most journals will list both, so include both. But write the collaboration name in the COLLABORATION field as "CMS" and not "CMS Collaboration", otherwise you will get "CMS Collaboration Collaboration" in the listing. However, if you are referencing a combined paper of CMS and another collaboration, you eliminate the COLLABORATION field and put the two collaboration names in the AUTHOR field. See the example of the BibTeX file for the combined Higgs paper with ATLAS (HIG-14-042) below.
7.  It is important that the list of authors for any reference is done correctly in the BibTeX file. Physical Review Letters requests that we include the names of all authors for a reference up to a maximum of 15 names. This is the highest value for any of the journals to which we submit papers, so we have taken this number as our internal CMS standard as well. THEREFORE, IF THERE ARE 15 OR FEWER AUTHORS FOR A REFERENCE, INCLUDE ALL OF THEIR NAMES IN THE BIBTEX FILE. If there are more than 15 authors, just give the first author's name plus "and others" in the BibTeX file. The individual journal's bibstyle file will then truncate the list at the appropriate number of authors for that journal. We have set our truncation limit to 1 author for our preprints. So in our preprints, if a reference has more than one author, only the first author and "et al." will be given. To make the process easier, we encourage you to take entries from the BibTeX files of our earlier papers. You can also use the INSPIRE search engine to find the full BibTeX entry for a reference. However, in this case, be sure to look up the actual published paper to make sure the title wasn't changed between the preprint and the publication. Also, convert the title to proper LaTeX format (e.g. change "sqrt(s)" to "$\\sqrt{s}$") if need be.
8.  Use only the URL field instead of embedding an href (this is a change from our previous practice).
9.  Decisions sometimes have to be made when you want to reference a paper that is on the arXiv preprint server but has not been published in a refereed journal. Detailed guidelines are given on the twiki [WhatCanBeQuoted](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/bin/view/CMS/Internal/WhatCanBeQuoted).
10.  Referencing standard MC programs. The authors of several of the more commonly cited MC packages have made specific requests concerning how we cite their work. See [this CMS twiki page](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/bin/view/CMS/CitationsForGenerators) for details.
11.  If you use PDFs in your paper, be sure to cite all the relevant publications, including, if applicable, a citation for the LHAPDF package. All the details regarding references of PDFs are available in [this CMS Twiki page](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/bin/view/CMS/PDFatCMS), which is regularly maintained by the SMP COM group. It should also be a good idea to check the [Twiki page of the GEN group](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/bin/view/CMS/CitationsForGenerators), given that there are correlations between generation tools and PDFs.
12.  The CMS Statistics Committee has a list of recommended references to cite for such statistical subjects as: Bayesian inference, confidence level criterion, likelihood ratios, Feldman-Cousins intervals, p-value and Z-value significances, and the Look-elsewhere effect. See their web page at: [Statistics References](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/bin/view/CMS/StatisticsReferences).
13.  We discourage using a URL as the sole reference to some documentation unless there is absolutely no place where the information has been published and it is a crucial element in your analysis. Also, keep in mind that some journals might not allow this.
14.  We strongly discourage using "Private communication" as a reference in our papers. The information obtained is obviously unrefereed, and thus its validity is in doubt. You should only use such a reference if the information obtained was substantial and vital to the results of the paper, as well as not being published anywhere. As an alternative, consider instead thanking the person in the acknowledgments of the paper.
15.  Our own BibTeX style file and those of most journals try to put all the words of a title except the first one in lowercase: use braces {} to protect words that must be capitalized, e.g. {LHC}, {TeV}, {CERN}, {CMS}.
16.  Do not put braces, {}, around the entire title since this will clash with the journal style.
17.  Journal names: include any journal series letter in the journal name, not in the volume number, e.g. journal = "Phys. Rev. D", volume = "65".
18.  Our standard CMS reference format and also the standard format for all the journals we use is to only give the beginning page number of any reference, not the first and last page numbers. So please follow this convention.
19.  Delete the issue number from the bibtex record.
20.  Please consult this [Twiki page](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/bin/view/CMS/Internal/PaperSubmissionPrepRefs) for more details on how to properly format the bibtex records.
21.  Note that most journals, such as JINST, reference their single-digit volume numbers without a zero in front (like 3 or 6). However, JHEP adds a zero to their single-digit volume numbers to make them always be 2 digits (like 03 or 06). So include this zero when referencing JHEP publications with single-digit volume numbers. Furthermore, JHEP makes all their article numbers (the last number in their references) 3 digits long by adding 1 or 2 zeroes, if necessary. So include these zeroes, if needed. Thus, a sample JHEP reference would look like: J. Smith, "Theory of Everything", JHEP 08 (2012) 003.
22.  COMMENTS can be placed outside the [@ENTRYTYPE![](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/pub/TWiki/TWikiDocGraphics/external-link.gif)](https://twitter.com/ENTRYTYPE){ ... } structures (unless JabRef is used--it will complain). ## Supplemental Material
In general, we discourage the presentation of results in a CMS paper in the form of an Appendix; it is usually better to have all the results presented in the body of the text. However, a paper with a large number of very similar figures or tables provides a more fluid reading if some of that material is placed in an Appendix. For most journals, where page limits are not strict, we recommend the use of a straightforward Appendix, which is an integral part of the refereed publication. When submitting a paper to Physical Review Letters (and Physics Letters B, to a lesser extent) the "appended material" can, alternatively, be provided as a separate "supplemental material" file, which will not appear in the printed journal, only in the online version, under an independent "tab".

The first question for you to answer is whether this additional material is really necessary for the scientific completeness of the paper. Keep in mind also that extra figures and tables can be approved and then placed on the public twiki page for the paper, where CMS speakers, for example, can obtain them for use in talks. If you do decide you have material that would best be presented in Appendix form, it is important to make sure the material is approved by the ARC and PAG conveners and is included as an appendix in all drafts of the paper, especially the draft used for the Collaboration Wide Review.

The distinction between an appendix and supplemental material is that an appendix is included with the paper and is used to distinguish between essential and less essential material. Supplemental material is provided in the article as a link to an independent document on the journal web site (they are responsible for it). It can be anything, animations, additional tables, whatever you like. Letter journals tend to allow supplemental info but not appendices. For nonletter journals (eg, long PRD papers) for textual material, an appendix is fine.

In the exceptional case that you cannot use a standard Appendix and, instead, you need to resort to the "supplemental material", you need to know that there will be two different formats for the supplemental material.

-   In the "CMS format", the supplemental material is included at the end of the paper as a LaTeX appendix. Thus, there is only one PDF file for the entire paper, which is used for the CWR and FR, and is submitted to CDS and to the arXiv preprint server. In this way, all of the results are in one file for the convenience of the reader.
-   In the "Publication format" of the paper, the supplemental material is a separate PDF file. The two PDF files -- one containing the usual text, figures, references, etc., and the other containing the supplemental material -- are then submitted to the journal. It is important to note that the "supplemental material” title must provide an informative description. For example, this is a suitable title: "Supplementary material: Centrality intervals and corresponding Npart values".

From the APS submission site: "the APS journals provide a platform for Supplemental Material (SM) deposited as electronic files. SM is useful information that is not essential to understanding an article’s main results. Being electronic, it can also include material that cannot at this time be integrated into the printed journal. Please note that SM cannot be used to avoid a length limit, since any article must be convincing without its SM." Note that APS supplemental material must be referenced through a citation—an example is also provided in the [Notes for Authors![](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/pub/TWiki/TWikiDocGraphics/external-link.gif)](https://gitlab.cern.ch/tdr/utils/raw/master/general/notes_for_authors.pdf) and that their specific requirements for supplemental material are given at [https://journals.aps.org/authors/supplemental-materials-journals![](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/pub/TWiki/TWikiDocGraphics/external-link.gif)](https://journals.aps.org/authors/supplemental-materials-journals).

We will use EXO-11-091 as an example of a paper with supplemental material. The supplemental material itself should be put into a LaTeX file called supplemental\_material.tex. The LaTeX file to generate the supplemental material in the CMS format should be called EXO-11-091\_supp.tex, with the conditional code to include the supplementary material in the main document as an appendix at the end of this file.

See [Ref. \[1![](https://twiki.cern.ch/twiki/pub/TWiki/TWikiDocGraphics/external-link.gif)](https://gitlab.cern.ch/tdr/utils/raw/master/general/notes_for_authors.pdf)\], Section 4.9, _Supplemental Material for Journals,_ for more details on what this LaTeX file should contain and the exact procedures to use for including supplemental material.

In the "Publication format", the two PDF files submitted to the journal are called EXO-11-091.pdf and EXO-11-091\_supp.pdf.

Carefully note how the supplemental material should be referenced within the main body of the text. For example, the APS specifies that the format for the reference in the text should be:

"See Supplemental Material at \[URL will be inserted by publisher\] for \[give brief description of material\]."

So you could write in the text of the paper:

"The results are available in tabulated form in \\suppMaterial",

where, \\suppMaterial has been defined in the EXO-11-091\_supp.tex file by the command:

```
\ifthenelse{\boolean{cms@external}}{\providecommand{\suppMaterial}
{the supplemental material [URL will be inserted by publisher]}}
{\providecommand{\suppMaterial}{Appendix~\ref{app:suppMat}}}
```

to be either the URL provided by the publisher in the "Publication format" case, or the appendix that contains the supplemental material in the "CMS format" case.

Other journals use a different procedure for including supplemental material. Consult with George Alverson if you are unclear on what to do to submit supplemental material to the journal for your paper.

## Updating a paper in GitLab and producing a PDF file for CADI
Changes to a paper draft should always be uploaded to GitLab and a new PDF version produced and stored in CADI. When a paper is about to be submitted, the authors should prepare a PDF version of the document to be submitted and make sure the version in GitLab is the latest one. The corresponding PDF file should always be stored in CADI. When changes to a paper draft are made because of referees' comments, again the revised version of the paper must be stored in GitLab and a new PDF file produced and stored in CADI. The final version of the paper accepted by the journal should always correspond to the latest version stored on GitLab and to the latest PDF file in CADI. However, when a journal makes changes to the format or conventions of a paper in order for it to conform to their style, do not make these changes to the version stored on GitLab or to the corresponding PDF file. Any version of the paper in GitLab and the accompanying PDF file in CADI should always keep the format and conventions described on this twiki page.
